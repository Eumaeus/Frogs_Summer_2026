Important to note: "To judge ancient acting by the standards of modern and Western acting is a mistaken proceeding." [^3] 

"Frogs could be played almost throughout by three actors: one taking the part of Dionysus, a second those of Xanthias and Aeschylus, and a third those of Herakles, Charon, Aiakos, the servant of Persephone, the Hostess, and Euripides; a fourth would be required for the very small parts (so far as spoken lines are concerned) of the Corpse, Plathane, and Pluton." Bare in mind comedies structure offered looser standards than the more composed and worthy tragedies. [^4] Actors had a certain amount of liberty in their roles. Chorus lines in comedy could be singing or speaking, "Aristophanes himself describes the chorus as 'speaking'." [^6] 

Actors were serious about their skill and presentation. Their rigor included actors' training as a performer, fasting/dieting, and practicing before their performances to "test" their voices making them ready to thrill the ever-demanding audiences. "The keen sensitiveness of the Athenian audience to the quality-especially, perhaps, the clearness-of the actor's voice is shown by the pains taken by the actor to come up to standard."  Audiences enjoyed "the imitation of animals and inanimate noises" hence their affinity for Aristophanes' *Frogs*, etc. Actors acted with gesture, but little is evidenced and it is likely to be less than modern days standards of performance. In the fifth century the texts of the plays seem to imply...a high degree of mobility, even rapid movement, kneeling, prostration, and a free play of gesture. ## Costumes/Masks

There is no written evidence of how costumes or masks were employed until Julius Pollux in the second century A.D. Evidence we have is found on objects and is not easily deduced to create a reliable interpretations of clothing beyond the regular like a himation. "Given the general convention of masks, if the audience is told that character is crying it will be prepared to accept the fact without demanding that the mask be obviously tear-stained." [^10] Since the actors are male masks were also necessary to denote gender. The wearing of a phallus also denoted gender but is generally only evidenced in comedies and not tragedies. [^11]

Archaeological evidence of costumes in comedy shows portrayal of animal choruses in the 6th century. [^13] Review figures!!! [^14] "It is clear from the literary evidence that Old Comedy enjoyed complete freedom in the production of masks to suit its characters." Clear evidence to masks that "burlesqued" of living people with "a degree of caricature." Very likely the chorus in *Frogs* wore frog-like masks. Also consider general distinctions made popular by comedy such as masks for "old man", "slave", "young woman", etc. [^15] What could be considered as the normal costume for Old and Middle Comedy is tights and phallos nd short chiton. [^16]

Clothing is evidenced to have been distinct to regularly played parts in plays: Old men wear a himation, and carry a crooked staff, younger men wear a scarlet garment (φοινικίς)  or a purple himation, rustics have a wallet, a stick, and a skin, normal young men wear red clothing, parasites wear black or grey, slaves wear a short white himation, old women wear green or light blue, priestesses wear white, mothers have a scarlet band round their heads, young women wear white or linen, heiresses wear white with fringes. "Costumes and tect could could go hand in hand in the essential task of communication, of telling the audience what it needed to know." Old Comedy utilized visual jokes and spectacle. [^17]

## Chorus/Music

"In comedy the chorus was a very important element down to the end of the fifth century...and in many plays the chorus simply sing interludes to break up the dialogue into scenes." [^18]. There were 24 members in an Old Comedy chorus. [^19] "As regards comedy, reference has already been made to the choruses partially disguised as animals, birds, or fishes, which figured frequently in the Old Comedy, and to the freedom of the poets to clothe their dancers in any guise which suited the play, and this freedom doubtless persisted to later periods, though in the Middle and New Comedy the grotesqueness was soon abandoned, and in so far as choruses appeared they appeared as ordinary human beings." [^20] "There is also little direct information as to the movements of the chorus in comedy, except in so far as the texts imply a great variety and freedom in the mode of entry, great liveliness during the play , and a marching departure, headed by a flute-player and varied at times by a vigorous dancing exit." [^21] Ancient Greek dancing has two main characteristics: the use of hands (χειρονομία) and an expressive or mimetic character of the performance. [^22] In comedy the dances both in singing choral odes and during the action must often have been livelier...though it can hardly be doubted that the incomparable lyrics of the Mystai in *Frogs* were wedded to equally lovely movement." [^23]

"Little use seems to have been made in the classical drama of instrumental music apart form words; but a few notes are sometimes interjected in comedy for special purposes-the song of the nightingale imitated on the flute in *Birds*, or the twanging of the lyre between the lines of Aeschylus a travestied by Euripides in *Frogs*. Such effects were termed διαύλιον or μεσαύλιον.
"To lead the singing, the first note (ἐνδόσιμον) was given not by an instrument but by coryphaeus, though it is to be feared that the start was sometimes assisted by the flute-player, not with his instrument only but with a wooden shoe (κρούπεζα) which he wore for the purpose. (Photius also speaks of a rattle.)" [^24]

## The Audience

"At the Lenaia only citizens and metics were present. The size of the audience at different periods cannot be precisely determined" [^25] There is reason to believe that the price of theater tickets was two obols at minimum. [^26] There was assigned seating for the Council "women were separate from men and courtesans from the other women, and it has been inferred from Aristophanes that women were seated at a distance from the skene." [^27] "With performances going on continuously from dawn to evening, the audience naturally provided itself with refreshments. Some may have left the theatre for a time and gone home for a meal-as Aristophanes suggest in *Birds*, it would be easy if they had wings; but there was also some circulation of provisions in the theatre itself." [^28]

"To explore in detail the general question of the relation between the political views of the audience and the political content of Old Comedy is beyond the scope." "But it is hard to steer a line between extreme views which, on the one side, see political propaganda as Aristophanes' principal interest and, on the other, view him as a pure dramatist with no ascertainable political views at all." [^29]

"Aristophanes may for his own purposes flatter the audience with an allusion, it might be not without a touch of irony, to their literary sense. He puts in the mouth of Aeschylus the sentiment that there were no judges of poets in the world like the Athenians, but this is counterbalanced by the appeal of Euripides in Hades to the baser elements in the crowd, and when Aristophanes flatters he is hardly to be taken more seriously than Demosthenes, who half a century later told the Assembly that no one was more acute than they." [^30]
